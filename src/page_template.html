2 · The business problem

A bio-processing investment fails or succeeds on three things that a spreadsheet hides.

Fragmented feedstock

Wood waste arises from hundreds of thousands of street trees and ad hoc storm and pruning work, then moves through six drop-off yards. Volumes are tracked by invoices, not by origin.

Margin erosion by distance

Chips are bulky (≈0.16–0.20 t/m³), so trucks fill on volume before weight. Today’s mulch hauls average ~120 km one-way; every extra km compounds fuel, driver time and emissions.

Uncertain carbon revenue

Return depends on carbon-credit and policy prices that move, on plant capex and on biochar sale price. The question is the break-even carbon price, not a single NPV.

5 · Strategic trade-offs & engineering rigor

Real data overturned a convenient assumption

The model assumed each ward ships to its nearest station. Against 2023 weigh-scale tonnage, Dufferin and Scarborough took ~65% of observed volume versus ~20% modelled (Spearman −0.43, n = 6; inconclusive statistically, unsupportive practically). With no origin field in the tickets, re-routing savings cannot be estimated, so the tool solves siting instead.

Volume and density matter as much as distance

Chips at 0.16–0.20 t/m³ fill a ~75 m³ trailer at roughly 12–15 t, about half the 25 t/truck v1 assumed. Emissions scale with m³ × km ÷ trailer volume, so density (+25% between the two values in the source sheet) and trailer size shift results as much as distance does. Sobol on transport emissions: return-leg treatment 0.60, fuel economy 0.25, trailer volume 0.14.
